Our Teachable Machine Project Statement

What 240 images taught us about building and questioning a model.

Group members: Adi Govil and Jake Derfel

Back to our Teachable Machine project

Our first working image classifier

For this project, we trained an image classifier to tell between mugs, bottles, and scenes without either object. We used Google Teachable Machine to train the model and added it to our LIS 500 website. This was our first time creating and training a working machine learning model using examples that we collected ourselves. Seeing it recognize an object in front of the camera made the ideas from class feel much more real world for us.

We were excited that the model worked well in our trials, but the activity also gave us a reason to ask what that success meant. A working demonstration shows that a model can perform a particular task under some conditions. We still need to explain those conditions and pay attention when the behavior changes. Connecting this experience to Joy Buolamwini's Unmasking AI helped us think about our responsibility as the people choosing the examples and presenting the results.

Our dataset and contributions

We collected 240 original images altogether. Both of us contributed images to all three classes:

  • Adi Govil: 40 mug images, 40 bottle images, and 40 empty-scene images, for 120 total.
  • Jake Derfel: 40 mug images, 40 bottle images, and 40 empty-scene images, for 120 total.
  • Combined: 80 mug images, 80 bottle images, and 80 empty-scene images, for 240 total.

Our custom training images were produced by us rather than downloaded or generated images. The Empty class represented scenes without either target object. Giving the model those examples allowed us to include removing the object as part of the demonstration.

Equal class counts made our dataset balanced in one clear way. However, the counts do not show how much variety the different images we have. Our object images showed the mugs and bottles centered and large in the frame. Even with 80 examples of each class, we were teaching the model through a fairly specific view of those objects. This later became important when we tried changing the distance from the camera.

Training and connecting the model

Teachable Machine made the training process much easier than we expected. We were especially surprised by how quickly it trained with our 240 images. We did not record an exact training time, but the process felt efficient and approachable for a first project. We could organize our examples, train the model, and see its predictions without first learning how to build a neural network ourselves.

Our webpage follows Daniel Shiffman's Coding Train example: load an exported model, classify camera images, receive a result, and display the label. The tutorial uses p5.js and ml5.js to connect these parts. [1] We adapted that structure for our model and website. The Start and Stop buttons let visitors control the camera, and the prediction appears as regular webpage text as well as on the canvas.

Google's explanation of transfer learning helps explain why this process can be fast. Teachable Machine reuses features learned by a pretrained model and trains a classifier for the new examples. [2] Our 240 examples were original, but the system did not learn everything from scratch using only those images. The assigned MobileNets paper describes efficient neural networks designed for devices with limited computing resources. [3] These resources helped us appreciate the existing research behind something that seemed so simple to use.

What we observed

In our informal trials, the model tells between the objects well even when the lighting was not very bright, as long as the object remained clearly visible. We found this encouraging because the demonstration did not seem to require especially bright lighting to work. However, we did not measure light levels or conduct a controlled lighting study. Our observation describes the situations we tried, rather than every possible dim setting.

Distance was the main factor we noticed affecting the predictions. Objects shown farther from the camera were less reliably recognized. Our explanation is that the centered, large objects in our training images made close-up views more familiar to the model. Moving an object farther away changes how much of the image it occupies and how much background surrounds it. This is a plausible explanation, but our informal testing does not prove the exact cause.

We added instructions asking visitors to hold the object closer to the camera. That helps people use the current model under conditions similar to its training examples. It also leaves us with an improvement to make: collecting examples at more distances and checking whether a revised model handles them better. Updating the instructions did not change what the existing model learned.

Connecting our choices to Unmasking AI

Buolamwini's idea of the coded gaze describes how discrimination and exclusion can become embedded in technology. Her work asks us to look at the people and decisions behind systems that may appear neutral. [4] Our project made the role of human choices easier to understand. We chose the labels, supplied the images, and decided which views of an object appeared in the training data. The model's limitations encouraged us to examine those choices instead of treating every unexpected result as an unexplained computer mistake.

A different connection appears in the opening chapter of Unmasking AI. Buolamwini describes her early curiosity about computers and learning HTML and CSS before moving toward other programming and robotics projects. [5] That progression felt relevant to this assignment. We started by building a website and are now using it to share a machine learning experiment. Being beginners does not prevent us from making something useful or asking serious questions about how it works.

Our mug and bottle classifier does not measure racial or gender bias, and its mistakes have very different consequences from errors in systems that evaluate people. Still, the exercise gave us practice noticing assumptions we had built into a dataset. In future projects involving people, we would need to ask whose experiences our examples represent and who could be harmed by an incorrect decision.

Looking beyond a successful demonstration

Buolamwini and Timnit Gebru's Gender Shades study provides a useful research connection. They evaluated commercial gender classification systems across combinations of skin tone and gender, finding substantial differences in performance between groups. [6] This shows why intersectional evaluation matters: a broad description of performance can leave important differences out of view. We should ask which groups are represented in an evaluation and whose errors become harder to see in a summary.

For our much smaller experiment, the practical takeaway is to describe performance under different conditions. Saying that our model works well would leave out the distance problem. Likewise, equal numbers of images in each class do not guarantee that the model has learned enough variation within those classes. We need to look beyond the total number of examples and consider what those examples actually show.

We have not established an overall accuracy percentage through a formal test. The score displayed beside a prediction is the model's score for that label, not a measured success rate across new images. The model also chooses among our three classes; an unfamiliar object can still receive one of those labels. A confident answer should give us a reason to check the result, rather than a reason to stop questioning it.

What we would improve and take forward

Our next improvement would be collecting new images with objects at different distances and positions. We would also include more variation in backgrounds and lighting. To evaluate whether those changes help, we would keep separate test images out of training and compare the original and revised models on the same examples. Recording the expected and predicted labels would let us describe errors more clearly than relying on memory from a live demonstration. These are proposed next steps, rather than tests we have already completed.

We are proud that our first model works and that both of us contributed equally to the original dataset. The speed and ease of Teachable Machine made us more interested in experimenting with machine learning. At the same time, the distance issue gave us a concrete lesson about the relationship between training examples and performance. As students studying data science, we want to carry that habit of questioning our data into future work. Building a model includes explaining its limits, listening when people report problems, and being willing to change the choices that produced those problems.
