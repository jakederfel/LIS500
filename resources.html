Implicit Bias Resource Guide

About this guide

College brings us into contact with people whose backgrounds and experiences may be different from our own. This guide introduces intersectionality, implicit bias, and implicit bias testing in everyday language. We want to help students think about how identities and learned assumptions can influence interactions, decisions, and opportunities.

These ideas also matter in technology. The people building a product make choices about whose needs to prioritize, what information to collect, and what counts as a successful result. Learning to question those choices is part of becoming a more thoughtful technology user and creator.

Intersectionality

What does it mean?

Intersectionality is a way of understanding how overlapping identities interact with systems of power and inequality. Race, gender, class, disability, and other parts of a person’s life do not operate separately. Kimberlé Crenshaw developed this framework to explain experiences that can be overlooked when discrimination is considered through only one category at a time.

Why does it matter?

An effort to include one group can still leave some of its members behind. For example, a networking event for women in technology might improve access for some students. However, an expensive attendance fee or an inaccessible venue could exclude low-income or disabled women. Looking only at gender would miss those barriers.

Our perspective

We think this is a useful reminder that people within the same category do not all have the same experience. In a data project, an overall average could make a service look successful while hiding difficulties faced by a smaller group. We should ask whose experience disappears when we summarize everyone together.

Implicit Bias

What does it mean?

Implicit biases are automatic associations or attitudes that can operate without our awareness. They can develop through repeated messages, social environments, and experiences. A person can consciously believe in fairness while still having associations that conflict with that belief.

Why does it matter?

These assumptions can affect how we interpret someone’s abilities or behavior. In a class project, a group might automatically give the coding role to one student and the note-taking role to another based on gender rather than asking about their skills and interests. Repeating that pattern could affect who gets opportunities to practice and improve.

Our perspective

Good intentions do not remove the need to examine our decisions. We think a useful starting point is asking what evidence supports an assumption. Before deciding that someone is the best person for a task, we can ask everyone about their experience and what they want to learn. That creates a fairer process than relying on first impressions.

Implicit Bias Testing

What does it mean?

Implicit bias testing, sometimes shortened to IBT, refers to methods used to examine automatic associations. One widely used example is the Implicit Association Test, or IAT. It compares how quickly someone sorts words or images when different categories share a response key.

Why does it matter?

A result can encourage reflection on associations we might not have noticed. However, it is not a diagnosis, a permanent label, or a reliable prediction of how one person will act in a particular situation. Results need to be interpreted carefully.

Our perspective

We would approach the test as a starting point for questions rather than a final judgment about someone’s character. What messages have we repeatedly encountered? What assumptions should we examine? Taking a test alone does not demonstrate that our decisions have become fairer; the follow-through matters.

Does an IAT result tell me whether I am a bad person?

No. A single score cannot summarize your character. You can reflect on the result while also looking at your actual behavior, listening to feedback, and using clearer standards when making decisions that affect others.

Putting these ideas into practice

  • Ask teammates about their interests before assigning roles.
  • Agree on evaluation criteria before reviewing someone’s work.
  • Consider cost, accessibility, and scheduling when planning student events.
  • When working with data, ask who is missing and whether group averages hide different outcomes.
  • Listen when someone describes a barrier you have not experienced yourself.
